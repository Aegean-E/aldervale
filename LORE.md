# The Aldervale Chronicle

> The canon of the vale. Every story change to ALDERVALE - quests, dialogue,
> names, bestiary, expansions - is built upon this document. Where the game
> and this document disagree, the game is a rumour and this is the truth.
>
> Third edition: the six hearths in full, the fall of Hollowmere, the Roll
> of Champions, the anatomy of an audit, the artifacts, the year-wheel, the
> sayings and songs of the vale, and the Act II treatment. The Chronicle
> adds pages when needed; so does this document.

---

*As inscribed by Elder Quill, Keeper-presumpt of Aldervale, in the three
hundred and ninety-ninth year of the Keeping:*

*The world is a page of light upon the dark, and we are the marks that move
upon it. The First Fire fell burning out of the elder sky, and where its
embers rested, the dark could not dry the ink. Seven sparks were gathered by
seven hands. Ours burns above the hearth of Aldercastle still. This is the
tale of the seventh spark, and of the price of its warmth, and of the knight
who came out of the Margins to argue with a debt.*

---

## The Cosmology: the Elder Sky and the First Fire

Before anything was written, there was only the Unwritten - a dark without
margin or page. Above the page hangs what the oldest records call the
**Elder Sky**. The Chronicle does not say what the Elder Sky is, and the
Chronicle is being careful: the stars, it notes, are distant lights that
have never needed tending, and it declines to call them hearths, and it
declines louder when asked what that implies. The shepherds call them
**the First Drafts** - worlds still burning, unedited, unkept. The Chronicle
has never corrected the shepherds. From the Elder Sky, at the beginning of
marking, fell the **First Fire**: not a sun, but the seed of one, shattering
as it fell. Seven embers survived the long burning-down. Seven valleys
caught them. Seven hearths were lit, and around each hearth a people
gathered, and the dark learned to keep its distance from the lighted places.

A hearth-stone is not a jewel, whatever the merchants of other vales say. It
is a **promise with a temperature**. It asks for nothing when lit - and
everything, eventually. A hearth-stone burns memory. Left unkept, it burns
what memory it holds and then gutters. Kept, it must be fed a Keeper: when
the Keeper dies, their spirit is bound into the stone at the shrine, and the
stone remembers them, and is warm because of them.

Aldervale's ember is the **Suncrest**. It has had eleven Keepings and twelve
souls. The first was Alder the First. The eleventh was Maren the Kind.

The lesson the Quiet Ages forgot is this: **warmth is never free. Someone is
always the wood.**## The Seven Hearths

The saga's map. Visit them sparingly, one at a time, and never all at once.

1. **Aldervale** - the **Suncrest**. Ours. The seventh ember, the warmest
   record, the tale you are reading.
2. **Hollowmere** - the **Mournstone**, now dark. A vale whose Keeping
   failed: its Keeper line broke, no successor was bound, and the stone
   burned its last memory in a single winter. What the Grey leaves behind
   is silence with buildings in it. Hollowmere's survivors walked out as
   the Unsworn. Its last guardian is a wyvern.
3. **Graywater** - the **Ledgerstone**. A vale that made its peace with the
   audit. Its people pay their warmth in written deeds: every kindness
   recorded, every cruelty deducted, and the Ledgerstone weighs the book.
   The Grey audits Graywater first, every time, and always finds it
   *adequate*. The Chronicle finds nothing about Graywater to like and
   everything to respect.
4. **Brackle** - the **Ironhearth**, kept by tournament. Each generation,
   Brackle's champions fence for the honour of being bound at death. They
   call it the Warm Victory and teach the forms to children. A dark mirror
   held up at arm's length.
5. **Sarles** - no stone at all. The **Chalkstone** was stolen three hundred
   years ago - the only hearth-theft before now. The thief walked into the
   Margins and never came out; Sarles survives by begging warmth off its
   neighbours, a nation of guests at other fires. Sarles keeps the only
   records of how a stone is carried. Corvin read them.
6. **Saltmere** - the **Brinestone**, drowned. Its people sank their own
   vale deliberately to hide from the Grey's first audit, and the stone
   obligingly learned to burn underwater. Its light is still seen far down,
   on still nights. The fishermen call it the Lantern and do not fish over
   it.
7. **The Marginal Hearth** - no agreed name. Every map of the elder sky
   shows a seventh ember that no living traveller has found. The Chronicle
   keeps a blank page for it, which is the only blank page the Chronicle
   permits itself.

## The Six Hearths Besides Ours

The index above is the map; this is the territory. Each vale is a caution, a
mirror, or a promise, and the saga will visit them one at a time.

### Hollowmere

The vale of the mere - a wide dark lake that swallowed its own lowlands in
some forgotten age, so that Hollowmere's people lived in two storeys: the
upper country of farms and forge-law, and the under-country of the archive,
built on the drowned shelf where the water is still and cold and keeps
things. Hollowmere trusted its book. That is the whole of its tragedy; the
rest is weather.

Hollowmere's rite was never carried in the head, as Wrenna the Binder
designed ours to be. Their priests read the binding from the **Second
Chronicle**, kept in the under-country's deepest room, and what is read can
drown. In the year 353 a winter fever broke Hollowmere's Keeper line - every
eligible soul in one season - and Elsbeth the Last, holding the Mournstone
steady with her own living warmth, sent her Champion to the mere to raise
the book before she died. The divers went down by lamplight. The cold of the
starving stone came up out of the ground to meet them, and the frost of a
dying hearth does what frost does in this world: **it unwrites.** The
Champion held the archive door through the last night, and what frost
touched, frost erased. In the spring divers brought up one book, whole and
dry and legible, and it was the wrong volume. The rites were in the second
book. The second book is still down there.

Elsbeth died unbound in early winter, because the words that might have
bound her were forty feet under black water in the wrong hands of the right
house. The Mournstone, with no new memory to hold, ate its last in a single
season. The Grey came, counted, found the account closed by default, and
took the warmth. It left the buildings. It took everything written into
them. The people who stayed too long forgot their own names; the people who
left in time left with their names and without their oaths, because oaths
are written things. Thus the Unsworn: not oath-breakers - the **oath-erased**.

And the Champion who held the door: the frost wrote over everything in the
under-country except where he stood. When Halvard the Borrower came, years
later, over the pass with his copying-kit, there was exactly one legible
page in the drowned archive - the Champion's own page in Hollowmere's
chronicle, framed in frost like a window. He copied it out word for word
into the margins of ours, against the winter. The page reads, in full:

> *Ser Alaric, of Hollowmere. He held the door.*

He is the only mark the frost refused. The Chronicle files this under
*evidence*, which is also where the Unsworn file their hope.

### Graywater

Graywater keeps the **Ledgerstone**, and has made an institution of being
found adequate. Its people wear their deeds stitched into their coat-linings
- good deeds in grey thread, which shows up in lamplight - and their
children are taught to leave every account square before sleep, because the
Ledgerstone weighs the book, and warmth is issued in the morning like
rations, which it is. The Grey audits Graywater first each cycle, the way a
careful inspector visits the tidy cupboard, and has never found the vale
wanting, and has never once been invited to stay. Graywater's embassy to
Aldervale, when it comes, will be polite, punctual, and quietly the most
frightening thing in Act IV. Their national saying is *be adequate*, and
they say it to children and to ambassadors in the same tone.

### Brackle

Brackle keeps the **Ironhearth** by tournament. When a Keeper's years run
low, the fences go up in the market square and the forms are taught openly
to whoever wishes to learn - the gamble being that anyone skilled enough to
win the binding is skilled enough to have enjoyed their life first. Brackle
calls the binding *the Warm Victory* and its champions die, on average,
delighted. The Chronicle finds nothing about Brackle to admire and nothing
to correct, which annoys it. In Act IV, Brackle's fencing masters would pay
a great deal to cross blades with the man who held the door at Hollowmere,
and Alaric should be allowed to lose to one of them on purpose, once, to see
how it feels to be beaten on a page where nothing is at stake.

### Sarles

Sarles has no stone. The **Chalkstone** was stolen three hundred years ago
by a thief whose name Sarles preserves with the care other nations reserve
for kings - Fenn the Quartered, who was not quartered, but was divided four
ways by grief, and walked into the Margins carrying the stone and never came
out. Sarles survives on begged warmth: treaty-coals from Graywater,
festival-fires from Brackle, and the quiet humiliation of a proud people
attending other nations' hearths as guests. Sarles keeps the only records in
the seven vales of how a hearth-stone is lifted, carried, hidden, and
sought - the **Carriage Papers**. Corvin read them the winter before the
theft, in the year 398, and signed the guest-book honestly, which the
Sarles archivists still mention. The Papers contain one line about what
happens to a stone whose Keeper dies unbound: *it sleeps, and may be
carried, and may be lit again by a new binding, at a hearth that will have
it.* Every exile in this saga, from Kestrel's Unsworn to the sleeping
Mournstone itself, eventually walks toward that line.

### Saltmere

Saltmere drowned itself on purpose, and the Grey's ledger still shows it as
*outstanding*. In the first audit of the elder cycle, the Salt-folk saw the
Counting coming and could not pass it, and would not fail it, and so they
opened their dikes in a single night and let the Underline in over the
fields, and the Brinestone obligingly learned to burn underwater. The vale
lives still, below, in the still cold brightness of the drowned country -
its windows are said to be lit, its suppers said to be warm, its people said
to be fine, and all of it forty feet down. The fishermen call the light the
Lantern and do not fish over it. The Grey has never found them. The Grey has
never stopped looking. Somewhere in the Unwritten there is a patient
clerk with one unpaid line, and this amuses the Salt-folk, who can hear the
auditors passing overhead like weather. Saltmere is the saga's proof that
the Grey can be dodged - and its warning that dodging is a kind of drowning.

### The Marginal Hearth

Nothing is written here, on purpose, by rule. The seventh ember appears on
every map of the elder sky and in no traveller's account. Pilgrims have
walked into the Margins looking for it; the Margins kept the pilgrims and
returned the rumours. The Chronicle's blank page for the Marginal Hearth is
the only page it refuses to fill, and Quill polices that refusal like a
debt. Act V ends wherever this paragraph stops being true.

## The Geography of the Page

Aldervale is an island of light in the **Underline** - the ink-dark sea.
The island is round because a hearth's protection is round, and where the
protection ends, the fog begins. The folk call the fog the **Margins**.
Beyond the Margins lies the **Unwritten**, where nothing has been marked yet
and nothing can live. Ships that sail into the Margins come back if they
come back at all, with crews who remember the voyage as a blank page.

- **The Village Green** - the heart of common life: the well, the shrine,
  the smithy, the fire that is never allowed to die entirely. Villagers
  sleep better within sight of the green. They are right to.
- **The Village Fire** - every hearth-fire in the vale is a **cutting** of
  the Suncrest, lit from the stone in the Founding. When the Suncrest was
  stolen, every cutting felt it. The village fire has burned grey at the
  roots since, and the villagers feed it anyway. It is the only fire in the
  world that is homesick.
- **The Inkwell** - the village well, dug in the Founding down to the water
  of the Underline itself. Its reflections arrive a moment late. Wishes
  dropped into it are heard somewhere, eventually, by no one who admits it.
- **Aldercastle** - raised in the Keeping to house and guard the Suncrest.
  Its keep's great hearth is the **Keep's Heart**, the stone's resting
  place. Its guards still swear to House Alder, a house with no living
  member, which the gate twins consider a technicality.
- **The Emberwood** - the eastern forest, grown thick where the ember fell.
  The ground there is thin: closer to the falling, closer to the burning.
  The oldest dead gather there, drawn to the warmth of the original site as
  moths to a window.
- **The First Camp** - the "old camp" of the eastern woods. Aldervale began
  here; when the castle was raised the people moved and left their first
  hearth to the trees. Its own fire - the eldest cutting - went out entirely
  on the night of the theft, and is now called the Dead Fire. Every Keeper
  makes a pilgrimage to this place once. It is where the Suncrest came from.
  It is where the Suncrest has been taken.
- **The Shrine** - older than the castle, younger than the camp. Its crystal
  is the **First Mark**: the point the Chronicle was begun from. It holds a
  small bright copy - a mark - of every soul that has prayed at it. When one
  of the marked falls in the vale, the light carries what remains of them
  home to be re-inscribed. The villagers call this grace. Quill calls it
  editing.
- **The Pass of Margins** - the only gap in the mountains, high in the
  north, where the fog thins enough to cross - some of the time, for some
  people. It is the place where the page curls. The Pass opens when the
  vale has need; the need chooses the traveller; the traveller rarely gets
  a say.

## The History: Six Ages

**I. The Falling.** The First Fire breaks apart in the elder sky. Seven
embers land. The Emberwood is the scar of ours.

**II. The Founding.** Alder the First, a shepherd with an unexplained gift
for finding things, carries the ember out of the woods and refuses to let it
be sold, split, or worshipped. He lights a hearth at the First Camp. People
gather. The dark pulls back from the valley like a tide going out.

**III. The Keeping.** Alder dies in the fourth winter. The stone dims the
night he is buried, and the vale's first wolves come down out of the pass,
walking like men who have forgotten how. The surviving elders, in terror,
perform the first binding: Alder's spirit is sealed into the Suncrest at the
shrine. The stone warms. The wolves fall. The **Keepers' Watch** begins: the
Chronicle is opened, the castle is raised over the Keep's Heart, and the
rule is written - *the Keeper serves the vale in life and warms it in death.*
The office of **Champion of the Keep** is created: one knight, sworn to
protect the Keeper's living days, never to outlive their duty by much.

**IV. The Long Winter.** In the ninety-ninth year, the cold collects. The
**Grey** - the Winter Court, the patient intelligence of the Unwritten -
arrives to inquire after a debt it believes unpaid: fire, it holds, is
stolen property, and hearth-stones are theft from the dark. The vale
endures a season that lasts two years. The Watch holds because the Keepers
of that era had been generous with their living years - the stone was fat
with memory. The Grey withdraws, and leaves the vale a receipt - see the
Fragments below - with the cadence now fixed in law and in fear: *it will
return each three hundred years, and each time it will count the fire and
count the debt.* The Quiet Ages are quiet because nobody does the
arithmetic. The arithmetic is now due.

**V. The Quiet.** Generations of ordinary warmth. The Chronicle grows. The
price of the stone fades into liturgy, then into rumour, then into a figure
of speech. House Alder dwindles to a name on a gate and then walks out of
it entirely. The Champion's oath becomes ceremonial. Keepers still go into
the stone, but the funerals are called "retirements", and the stone's new
warmth each time is called "a blessing of the season."

**VI. The Fading (the present).** Maren the Kind, the eleventh Keeper, dies
in her sixty-first year and is bound according to the rite. Her husband -
Ser Corvin, Champion of the Keep, the man who carried her to the shrine
because he refused to let the priests touch her - stands witness. He later
testifies, to no one who wanted to hear it, that as the rite closed he felt
her **stop**, the way a voice stops: not ended - *filed*. He asks the
Chronicle's archivists to show him the pages that say where the kept souls
go. The pages say nothing. There are no pages. He asks what the Grey will
find when it next counts the fire. The answer is a polite silence, because
the audit is three hundred years on schedule and one season away.

Ser Corvin gives the vale one winter of warning. Then he takes the Suncrest
from the Keep's Heart, takes the Keeper's circlet from the shrine vault,
reads everything Sarles kept about carrying stones, walks east into the
Emberwood, and does not come back.

## The Present Crisis

The vale knows only this: the amulet is gone, the Captain wears a stolen
crown, and the dead walk at night. Here is what is actually true.

Corvin carried the Suncrest to the First Camp - where the ember began - to
perform the **Rite of Return**: an unbinding, worked at the place of
beginning, that would open the Suncrest and let every kept soul go out,
including Maren, and then give the ember back to the sky, closing the debt
and ending the Watch forever. The rite is slow. It requires the stone to sit
unkept while the binding is unpicked, strand by strand, in the order they
were laid - Alder first, Maren last. Sarles's records say a carried stone
can be hidden from the Grey for a while. Nothing says it can be hidden from
the dead.

While the Suncrest is unkept, its warmth gutters. The vale's dead - not
evil, only **unfinished** - feel the fire flicker and walk toward it, as
they have always walked toward warm things. The skeletons at the First Camp
are not Corvin's army. They are a siege he is enduring. He holds the
rite-circle with a greatsword and a dead man's patience, night after night,
killing things he believes he is trying to free.

And the circlet - the instrument of binding, forged by Bram to the old
specifications - has begun to do what instruments of binding do. Starved of
a proper Keeper, it has started binding the man wearing it. Ser Corvin went
into the Emberwood to unpick the Watch; the circlet, strand by strand, is
knitting him into it. He no longer bleeds, no longer sleeps, and no longer
certain which of these the rite did and which the crown did. The tragic
arithmetic of the Ashen: **to free his wife he is becoming the thing he set
out to destroy.** The circlet chooses. It has always chosen. It is choosing
him.

The vale, reading darkness by firelight, sent for a hero.

Elder Quill - Keeper-presumpt, Maren's chosen successor, the next wood for
the fire - sent for **Ser Alaric**.

## Dramatis Personae

**Ser Alaric, the Margin Knight (the player).** Walked in through the Pass
of Margins one grey morning with a serviceable sword, a gentleman's manners,
and no past he can produce on request. The shrine marked him on arrival;
when he falls, the First Mark carries him home. What Quill has not told
him: the Pass opens for the vale's need, and the vale's need was specific.
Keeper Halvard the Borrower, Quill's teacher, copied a figure out of a
drowned chronicle and kept the copy in the margins of our own - a hero from
a vale that no longer exists, held in reserve against a winter. Alaric is a
re-inscription. He does not know which vale he was first written in. The
truth waits in Act III: he was Hollowmere's Champion, and Hollowmere's
Champion *failed*. When the Mournstone starved, Alaric was holding the
door. He is not a chosen one; he is somebody's second draft, and the saga
will never let destiny be a substitute for that. He fights as if he
remembers being better at it once. He was. The full account of the door he
held is told under Hollowmere, in the Six Hearths.

**Elder Quill, Keeper-presumpt.** Chronicler, widower of no one, husband of
the vale. Carries the staff whose crystal is a chip of the First Mark.
Writes the Chronicle nightly, and knows - has always known - what the next
chapter of the Keeping costs, because it is his name in it. Sent Alaric to
retrieve the Suncrest for the reasons he gave, and for one he did not: as
long as the stone sits unkept at the First Camp, the rite can be finished.
As soon as it is home above the Keep's Heart, every kept soul - Alder's,
Maren's, all twelve - is bound the deeper, and Quill's eventual binding
becomes a matter of correct paperwork. He is kind to Alaric. He is kind to
everyone. It is the only vice he permits himself. Each year his
staff-crystal burns a shade dimmer, and he writes a little smaller.

**Maren the Kind, the eleventh Keeper.** Died in her sixty-first year, in
her own bed, in a good autumn, and went to her binding *by choice* - Quill
had asked her, gently, months before, and she had said yes the way she said
yes to everything: immediately, and then worried about it quietly for
weeks. Corvin never forgave the yes. The Chronology records that he did not
forgive Quill either. This is the cruellest weight on the Captain: he is
not only defying the Watch; he is overriding his wife's own last gift. If
the rite frees her, it unfrees her from a promise she made with her whole
heart. The Chronicle keeps her page as a single line - *she asked for a
smaller funeral than the rite allowed* - and has never been able to close
it.

**Ser Corvin the Ashen, the Bone Captain.** Champion of the Keep, twice
over: first by oath, now by obstinacy. The circlet he wears is the Keepers',
not the lord's - the guards mistake it for the crown of dead House Alder,
and he has stopped correcting them, partly from contempt and partly because
the circlet has begun to correct him. He is not raising the dead; he is
refusing to lower his sword until his wife is free. He will ask Alaric, at
the point of a greatsword, one question the game has not yet let anyone
answer: *"You have seen what the warmth is made of. Whose name will you be
wearing in it?"*

**Bram the Smith.** Forged the Keeper's circlet at the castle forge in the
year 382, to the old specifications, and only later asked the archivists
what the specifications were *for*. He did not like the answer. He moved
his anvil to the village the following spring and has not entered the
castle since. He watched his mother Collen bound into the stone when he was
fourteen, and remembers the warmth that winter as the last honest one. He
will not speak of the circlet. He pays generously for wolf pelts, sells
steel at a fair price, and hammers very late at night, because Bram has
begun - quietly, without telling anyone - to forge a **second thing**: a
lantern, a chronicle-box, a vessel meant to hold a hearth-stone *without
binding anyone*. Metal remembers, he says, and means it technically. The
lantern is nearly finished. It will work exactly once, at the worst
possible moment.

**Vesper, the Mourning Wyvern.** Not of this vale. A wyvern bonds to a
hearth in its first year of life and outlives it by centuries; Vesper's
hearth was the Mournstone of Hollowmere, and Vesper was elsewhere the
winter it starved, which is the whole of her tragedy in one clause. She
carries a shard of the dead stone in her claws and cannot put it down. She
came over the pass years ago and has circled Aldercastle ever since,
because she can smell a hearth burning down to its memories, and she cannot
tell the difference between a stone that is dying and a stone that is being
opened. She is not hunting. She is **keeping vigil for the wrong grave.**
She has never once attacked the village; she landed at the castle gate
once, and the gate twins still argue about whether it was a bow. The
chickens do not believe any of this. Wren the Herder believes all of it.

**The gate twins, Ser Aldous and Ser Aldric.** The castle's two guards,
sworn to dead House Alder, who may be the last of that line - Aldera the
Mapless walked out of the vale in 212, and Alder blood is like that: it
goes where the page is thin. The twins have independently decided never to
ask each other. They maintain the legend of the house, the gate, and the
hearth with equal gravity, and they are the only guards in the seven vales
who salute a fireplace.

**The villagers.** Mira of the Mill remembers when the Suncrest hung above
the Keep's Heart and the castle doors stood open - warmth makes a people
generous, and its absence makes them careful; she noticed the east camp's
fire die first, because she watches horizons. Old Tam is older than he
admits, claims kin to the ninth Keeping, and calls the Fading "the Winter
counting again"; nobody listens, because Tam also blames the miller's
weathervane for things. Wren the Herder talks to chickens and is right
about the wyvern. Each of them holds one true page of the story. The vale
is a chronicle that has been read aloud so many times it believes only the
comfortable pages.

**Kestrel of the Unsworn (the road bandits).** Hollowmere's diaspora: men
of the unlit vale, cold by policy rather than by nature. They camp the warm
roads between village and camp because cold men follow warmth the way the
dead do. Kestrel keeps them from the worst of it - she has rules: no
children robbed, no fires quenched, no offerings at the First Camp touched
- and she is holding her people together on the hope of a hearth somewhere
that will take them. They are not bandits by calling. They are guests whom
no fire will seat.

**The Grey (the Winter Court).** Not a monster. An **auditor**. The
Unwritten's patience given a schedule. It returns each three hundred years
to count the fire against the dark's ledger, and its coming solstice is the
clock under the whole tale. The Grey does not hate warmth. It objects to
warmth's accounting. Every soul bound into a hearth-stone is, in its
ledger, a debt recorded as an asset. The Rite of Return would close the
account honestly - which is why the Grey has not interfered with Corvin
yet, and why the honest ending frightens Quill more than the dark does.
The Grey's heralds are not monsters either: an early frost, a wolf walking
wrong, a winter that arrives a week early and apologizes for nothing.

## House Alder and the Champions

**House Alder** is not a dynasty of kings; it never needed to be, because a
house that owns the fire owns the room. Founded by Alder the First, it
supplied keepers of hearths and gate-wards and one memorable tax assessor,
and dwindled across the Quiet Ages from a great house to a small stubborn
one to a name on the gate. Its sigil was a tree with the roots above the
branches - the family joke being that the Alders knew which way warmth
flows. The last of the line, **Aldera the Mapless**, walked into the
Margins in the year 212 carrying the household cutlery and no explanation;
she is called the Mapless because she left every map the house owned pinned
to the gate, facing outward, *as if for someone coming the other way.* The
gate twins keep the maps. They have never said so. The Chronicle numbers
the Champions it chooses to, which is all of them who mattered and none of
them who were merely brave.

**The Champion's oath, as sworn at the shrine:**

> *I am the hand before the fire.*
> *My Keeper's days are my length of days.*
> *When the page takes them, I follow soon,*
> *that no warmth want for a witness.*

In the early Keepings, Champions kept that oath literally - they followed
their Keepers within the year, some say by grief, some by arrangement, and
the Chronicle records both without choosing. In the Quiet Ages the oath
went ceremonial and Champions began living on, and the Chronicle pretended
not to notice. Ser Corvin has now outlived Maren's binding by a year and
counting, because he has something to do, and the Chronicle wishes him rest
without specifying which kind.

**The Roll of Champions:**

1. **Ser Berrin the Wall** - first of the line; served Wrenna the Binder;
   followed her within the year, on schedule, by oath. His shield hangs in
   the shrine and is the only object in the vale the frost has never
   touched. Nobody has tested this recently. Nobody should.
2. **Ser Hult the Halfsworn** - broke his oath and walked into the Margins
   rather than follow his Keeper. The Chronicle keeps his page open to this
   day, which is not sentiment: an open page is a debt, and the Grey counts
   open pages.
3. **Dame Ferris the Faithful** - married her Keeper, with the shrine's
   blessing and the archivists' disapproval; served her in both capacities
   and followed her in neither, being the first Champion to petition for -
   and win - the right to outlive. The Chronicle records the marriage and
   nothing else about it, which is the Chronicle being discreet. Corvin and
   Maren leaned on Dame Ferris's precedent. Only one of them read the
   footnotes.
4. **Ser Osney the Unhurried** - held the First Camp against a rising of
   the unfinished dead for forty nights, alone, on the reasoning that "the
   dead are not in a hurry either." His account of that siege is why
   Corvin knew the camp could be held. The account's last line - *the trick
   is to be sorrier than they are* - is taught to no one and remembered by
   everyone.
5. **Ser Katrin the Listener** - the only Champion who pressed her ear to
   the Suncrest and told the truth about what she heard. Declared mad;
   pensioned; her testimony is one of Guil the Doubtful's missing pages,
   which the Chronicle calls a coincidence with a straight face.
6. **Ser Corvin the Ashen** - bound to no lie: served Maren the Kind in
   life, failed to prevent her keeping, and has outlived the oath's year by
   three hundred days and counting. The page is open. The ink is wet.

*For the Champion of Hollowmere, see the margin note - one page, held in
the Borrower's hand, water-stained, framed in frost, legible. It says only:
he held the door.*

## Anatomy of an Audit

The Grey is not weather, though it uses weather. An audit has stages, and
the vale has learned to read them the way sailors read a swell.

**The heralds.** First an early frost, right on the edge of ordinary. Then
wolves walking wrong - upright, at dusk, looking at things wolves do not
look at. Then a winter that arrives a week early and does not apologize.
None of these are the Grey. They are the page curling before the ink
arrives.

**The Counting.** On the midwinter solstice, the Grey comes to the shrine
as stillness with a presence in it, and counts. It counts the fire: every
memory the stone holds, weighed. It counts the debt: every soul held
against its will or otherwise, entered as liabilities recorded as assets,
which is the specific entry the Grey exists to resent. It reconciles. A
passed audit leaves a receipt - frost-nailed to the castle gate, in ledger
hand - and a century of ordinary winters. The Long Winter's receipt is
preserved in the Fragments, word for word.

**Repossession.** A failed audit is not a battle. The Grey has never taken
a life; it has taken everything else. It unwrites: warmth first, then
purpose, then name, then oath, then the particular silence a room has when
people who loved each other have left it. Hollowmere's buildings still
stand because buildings are not written things - everything written *into*
them is gone. There is no fighting this. There is only passing the audit,
hiding from it (ask Saltmere, forty feet down, laughing), or - the third
way, unproven, the way the saga is walking toward - showing the auditor a
fire that owes nothing and letting the ledger find nothing to resent. The
Grey is not cruel. It is punctual. The Chronicle, which is the closest
thing the vale has to a second opinion, notes that of all the foes in this
world, only the Grey has never once lied.

## The Suncrest Itself

A disc of gold the size of a spread hand, warm as a held bird, always a
shade warmer than whoever holds it - which is the first clue, if anyone
thought about it, that it is holding *them*. Eleven Keepings and twelve
souls are bound in it, strand over strand, Alder at the centre. The warmth
you feel through the metal is not radiance. It is **regard**. Old Tam is
right: the vale was warm when it hung above the hearth. The vale was also,
in the strictest sense, being worn.

## Artifacts of the Vale

**The Keeper's circlet.** Forged by Bram in the year 382, to the old
specifications: seven bands of quenched steel over a core of Alder-wood
from the First Camp, sized - this is the detail Bram did not ask about -
to the skull of a willing donor. It is the instrument of binding, the
needle of the Watch, and it has one property the specifications do not
mention because the specifications assumed nobody would be wearing it
casually: **it chooses.** Starved of a Keeper, it begins binding whoever
bears it. It chose Maren, in her hour, cleanly. It is choosing Corvin now,
slowly, out of spite or patience - the Chronicle cannot tell which, and
suspects the circlet cannot either. After the Captain's fall it sits in
the shrine vault, warm, patient, and increasingly interested in the
margin knight. Bram could unmake it; Bram will not, because metal
remembers, and what the circlet remembers is twelve souls, and unmaking
it would be twelve funerals held at once.

**Quill's staff.** Alder-wood, iron-shod, tipped with a chip of the First
Mark that Halvard passed to Quill with the words *write smaller than I
did.* The chip glows in proportion to what remains of Quill. It is the
saga's most honest gauge: when the staff goes dark, the next Keeper's page
opens, and everyone in the village knows how to read it, and everyone
pretends not to.

**Vesper's shard.** A splinter of the Mournstone, cold as a debt, carried
in the wyvern's foreclaw since the fall of Hollowmere because putting it
down would feel like burying the hearth a second time. The shard wants to
go home. Near Hollowmere's mere it hums low enough to feel through a
saddle, and it points - Vesper swears this by circling tighter - toward
the shelf where the Second Chronicle lies. It is, in the strictest sense,
a compass made of grief.

**The Chronicle.** Bound in boards of Alder-wood cut from the First Camp's
first fallen tree; written in ink drawn from the Inkwell's deepest bucket;
begun at the First Mark in the year 9. Its rules, as far as any archivist
has dared establish: it cannot be torn (attempted during Guil's doubt; the
attempter is not recorded; see the Ninth Keeping); it adds pages when
needed, and only then; it cannot lie, but it lies politely - by omission,
by redaction, by the placement of a comma in the marriage record of Dame
Ferris - and its last line is blank, and Quill refuses to write it, and
has refused for forty years. The Chronicle is the saga's second
protagonist. Everything warm in this world is a story being spent; the
Chronicle is where the spending is written down.

**The Lantern.** Bram's second forging, secret, unnamed by its maker's
own rule - *you don't name a thing that might not work; you name the grave
if it doesn't.* A cage of quenched steel around a cradle of polished
circlet-scrap, built to hold a hearth-stone the way a hand holds water:
completely, and without gripping. If it works, a stone could be kept
unkept - warm, carried, and owing no one. If it works. Bram tests it on
cuttings, at night, alone, and has not yet told anyone what the results
are, which is itself a result.

## A Bestiary of the Vale (partial, honest)

- **Restless bones (the unfinished dead).** The dead of Aldervale are not
  evil; they are unfinished, and unfinished things walk toward warmth
  trying to finish. The eldest of them gather in the Emberwood, by the
  First Camp, where the ground is thin. A mason found frozen mid-gesture
  there was completing a wall no one can see. They are stronger after
  sundown, because the unkept stone gutters then and whatever the stone
  holds down gets an hour of slack.
- **Winter wolves.** Not the Grey's - the Grey has no need of teeth. The
  wolves are the vale's ordinary grief, amplified: the Fading pulled the
  stone's warmth back from the treeline, and the treeline moved with it.
  They remember being something else; on the coldest nights they walk on
  their hind legs to see farther, and the shepherds shoot them anyway, and
  the wolves understand.
- **The Unsworn.** See Kestrel, above. Robbery is what cold does when it
  is polite.
- **Chickens.** Ordinary, except in proximity to Vesper, when they become
  prophets. Nobody has written this down but Wren, and Wren's notes are
  mostly feed schedules, which is also a kind of prophecy.

## Songs of the Vale

**"The Counting"** - a skipping rhyme of the vale, sung since the Long
Winter:

> First comes frost and then comes the grey,
> count the fire, count the day;
> hide a coal in your pocket, hold a word in your chest,
> and the Winter goes counting east to west.

*The Chronicle notes that children have sung this for three hundred years,
and considers it probably healthy.*

**"The Champion at the Door"** - a song of the Unsworn, sung low, around
fires they are not allowed to keep:

> The stone grew cold in the darkening mere,
> and the book went down where the ink runs deep,
> and the Champion stood at the archive door
> while the frost wrote over the vale's own sleep.
> Hold the door, said the light, till the morning mark;
> a hand is a hinge, and a hinge holds fast;
> hold the door through the cold and the dark -
> some pages are saved by being held, at last.

*Alaric has hummed this without knowing where he learned it. The Chronicle
files this under evidence.*

## The Year-Wheel

The vale's calendar, and the quest hooks that grow on it.

- **First-Thaw** (early spring) - the Inkwell is drawn and the first
  bucket poured at the roots of the village fire: *waking the cutting.*
  The fire is inspected for grey. Everyone pretends not to look at the
  east.
- **Sheep-Count** (late spring) - Wren's festival. Everything is counted,
  including the chickens, twice. The one year the count came out even,
  the vale declared it an omen and nothing happened, which Wren calls
  proof.
- **The Longest Afternoon** (summer solstice) - the safe hours. Oaths are
  sworn, contracts signed, and quarrels settled while the page is
  brightest; it is the only day the Unsworn are traditionally let across
  the river, on the reasoning that daylight is everyone's.
- **The Falling** (autumn equinox) - lanterns floated on the Inkwell, one
  per name, for the dead. The lanterns drift to the same spot and hold
  there, in a slow wheel, until they go out together at dawn. Nobody has
  ever retrieved one. Nobody has wanted to.
- **Keeping Day** (early winter) - the Chronicle is read aloud at the
  shrine from dusk to dawn, every Keeper's page in order. Quill has never
  once finished before dawn and never once stopped early. The reading is
  the vale's audit of itself, and the tradition insists that the Grey,
  if it is anywhere near, listens.
- **Hearth-Nights** (midwinter solstice) - the Twins' festival and the
  marriage season, held at the shrine on the coldest night: *married on
  the coldest night, to spite the arithmetic.* The same night, once every
  three hundred years, is the Counting. The vale has always known this
  and has always married anyway, which either proves courage or proves
  that courage is a kind of arithmetic.

## The Five Acts

The saga's shape. Each act keeps a promise and collects a cost.

**Act I - The Hearth-Stone (the current game).** A knight out of the
Margins retrieves the Suncrest from the grieving Captain. Promise: the
vale rewarmed, the hero named. Cost: the deeper binding, and the rite
broken with strands still in it - among them Corvin's own, taken by the
circlet against his will. Act I ends with Quill closing the Chronicle in
a smaller hand than he opened it.

**Act II - The Keeper's Price.** The circlet, recovered from the Captain's
fall, sits in the shrine vault and is warm; Quill will not touch it; the
circlet chooses, and it has begun to choose Alaric. Alaric learns what the
victory re-bound - twelve souls, knitted deeper, Maren among them - and
finds pages in the Chronicle that mention him. Bram's lantern nears
completion: a vessel to hold a hearth-stone without binding anyone. Vesper
lands nearer the village each week. Promise: a way to keep a hearth without
a soul. Cost: someone must be the first to hold the lantern when it is
tested, and the test is the audit.

**Act III - The Wyvern's Errand.** Win Vesper's trust - she judges by
warmth of intent, not words, and she has been wrong exactly once, fatally.
Carried over the pass to Hollowmere: the drowned chronicle, the silent
vale, the Unsworn's lost home, and the truth of Alaric's first draft. He
was Hollowmere's Champion, and he held the door while the Mournstone
starved, and the Borrower copied him out of the wreckage of that page
because he was the only mark still legible. Promise: the truth of Alaric.
Cost: the truth of Alaric - and Kestrel's people waiting for the vale to
answer whether a hearth can be shared.

**Act IV - The Winter Court.** The solstice of year 399. The Grey arrives,
counting. Graywater's embassy offers Aldervale the Ledgerstone's solution -
pay in recorded deeds, be found adequate - and the vale must refuse it.
The answer is the Chronicle itself, read aloud at the shrine until the
First Mark burns: a tale is a small sun, and a chronicle honestly kept,
generously told, and often re-read is a fire that owes nothing. Bram's
lantern carries the Suncrest unbound above the assembly. The fire of the
Chronicle burns the only fuel the vale will not miss: its comfortable
lies. Promise: warmth that costs no one. Cost: the comfortable page.

**Act V - The Margins.** What Quill has refused to write for forty years
is the Chronicle's last line, held blank. Beyond the fog: Aldera the
Mapless and where she went; the Marginal Hearth, the seventh ember no
traveller has found; the Grey's superior, if such a thing is written
anywhere; and the place where drafts go when they are not re-inscribed.
Alaric - the second draft who saved the page - walks to where the page
curls, and the saga ends the only way its rules allow: one question
answered, two opened, and the line finally written. Promise: the page
turned. Cost: the pen.

## Act II in Detail: The Keeper's Price

The pattern for act treatments. Acts III-V await theirs in the same shape:
zones, quests, reveals, end state, promise and cost.

**Zones.** The village and shrine as known; the shrine vault (new); Bram's
night forge (the village smithy after midnight); the Emberwood's western
edge and the First Camp in its new aftermath - the rite-circle broken,
the Dead Fire's ash ring still warm in one place, which nobody can explain.

**Quests.**

1. *The Warm Circlet.* The vault circlet warms whenever Alaric passes the
   shrine steps. Quill forbids the vault. Bram, asked indirectly, says
   only that metal remembers, and hammers louder. The quest is not to take
   the circlet; it is to learn why everyone who loves Alaric is suddenly
   careful with their hands around him.
2. *The Smaller Hand.* Fetching archive volumes for Quill, Alaric reads
   Halvard's margins and finds himself: one water-stained page, and the
   Borrower's hand underneath it, and the words *against the winter.*
   Quill, cornered, tells the truth with his usual kindness, which is to
   say, the part of it that can be borne that evening.
3. *Vesper's Vigil.* The wyvern's trust is won by carried warmth: bring a
   lit cutting to her perch and shield it in the wind with your body. She
   watches the flame, not the knight. Three nights of this and she lands
   on the green, and the chickens are vindicated, and Wren weeps, and the
   shard in her claw hums - low, and to the east.
4. *The Unsworn's Fire.* Kestrel asks for a hearth-cutting for her camp.
   Quill cannot spare one: every cutting dims the stone's reach, and the
   stone is one season from an audit. The choice stands: warmth for
   guests, or warmth kept for the counting. Neither answer is wrong, and
   the Chronicle will record whichever one hurts.
5. *The Night Test.* Bram, finally, shows someone the Lantern. One
   cutting, one night, in the cage: if the cutting burns till dawn without
   dimming the stone, the principle holds. It does. Bram does not name it.
   Bram says: *"All right. Now the big one."* - and the audit is one
   winter away, and this is where Act II ends.

**Reveals.** Alaric's origin (told gently, by Quill); the circlet's
interest in Alaric (shown, not explained); the Lantern's principle
(proven on a cutting); Vesper's trust (won); Kestrel's cause (humanized);
the true cost of Act I's victory (understood by the player as a feeling
before it is ever said aloud by anyone).

**End state.** The Watch is not ended, but for the first time in four
hundred years there is a plan that is not a funeral. Quill writes a page
in a hand very slightly larger than the year before. Vesper sleeps on the
green. The circlet, in its vault, is patient the way instruments of
binding are patient. And on the last night, Alaric - checking the Lantern
alone - hears, from inside the vault, very quietly, the circlet humming
the tune the Unsworn sing about him.

**Promise kept:** a way to hold fire without gripping it exists.
**Cost collected:** the circlet has chosen, and it chose the man who
cannot be spent, because he is only a copy - and the Chronicle's margin
now carries a mark beside his name that Quill will not explain.

## The Theme, Stated Once

Every fire in this world is a story someone is being spent to tell. The
Chronicle's answer - the saga's destination, held for the final act - is
that a hearth could be fed something else: that a **tale is a small sun**,
and a chronicle honestly kept, generously told, and often re-read might warm
a vale without costing anyone the way Alder was cost, and Wrenna, and
Maren, and Quill. The last act of ALDERVALE, whenever it is written, is the
vale learning to burn its stories instead of its people - and the first
proof that it works is the game itself: a world made of nothing but marks,
warm because someone keeps writing it.

## Rules for Future Story Writing

- Keep the fable cadence: short declarative sentences, one image per line,
  no explanations offered twice.
- Draw names from the lexicon of the page: mark, margin, quill, ember, ash,
  chronicle, strand, ledger, ink, vigil, draft, cutting, gutter. Nothing is
  named accidentally.
- Never fully explain the glyphs. The world being made of tiny marks of
  light is canon, numinous, and not up for mechanistic revision.
- No gods. The Elder Sky is rumour at best. No chosen ones either: Alaric
  is a draft, not a destiny; the vale's need is real, and his appointment
  is editorial.
- Every quest answers one question and opens two.
- The dead are not evil; they are unfinished. Every villain is someone's
  grief with a sword in it. The Grey is not cruel; it is punctual.
- The seven hearths are the saga's map; visit them sparingly and one at a
  time, and keep the Marginal Hearth unwritten until the end.
- Warmth, memory, and story are the same thing wearing three coats. When
  in doubt, the scene is about which one is being spent.
- The comfortable page is allowed to be true, and never allowed to be the
  whole truth.
