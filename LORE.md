# The Aldervale Chronicle

> The canon of the vale. Every story change to ALDERVALE - quests, dialogue,
> names, bestiary, expansions - is built upon this document. Where the game
> and this document disagree, the game is a rumour and this is the truth.

---

*As inscribed by Elder Quill, Keeper-presumpt of Aldervale, in the fourth
hundredth year of the Keeping:*

*The world is a page of light upon the dark, and we are the marks that move
upon it. The First Fire fell burning out of the elder sky, and where its
embers rested, the dark could not dry the ink. Seven sparks were gathered by
seven hands. Ours burns above the hearth of Aldercastle still. This is the
tale of the seventh spark, and of the price of its warmth, and of the knight
who came out of the Margins to argue with a debt.*

---

## The Cosmology: the First Fire and the Seven Hearths

Before anything was written, there was only the Unwritten - a dark without
margin or page. Into that dark fell the First Fire: not a sun, but the seed
of one, shattering as it fell. Seven embers survived the long burning-down.
Seven valleys caught them. Seven hearths were lit, and around each hearth a
people gathered, and the dark learned to keep its distance from the lighted
places.

A hearth-stone is not a jewel, whatever the merchants of other vales say. It
is a **promise with a temperature**. It asks for nothing when lit - and
everything, eventually. A hearth-stone burns memory. Left unkept, it burns
what memory it holds and then gutters. Kept, it must be fed a Keeper: when
the Keeper dies, their spirit is bound into the stone at the shrine, and the
stone remembers them, and is warm because of them.

Aldervale's ember is the **Suncrest**. It has had eleven Keepers. The first
was Alder the First. The eleventh was Maren the Kind.

The lesson the Quiet Ages forgot is this: **warmth is never free. Someone is
always the wood.**

## The Geography of the Page

Aldervale is an island of light in the Underline - the ink-dark sea. The
island is round because a hearth's protection is round, and where the
protection ends, the fog begins. The folk call the fog the **Margins**.
Beyond the Margins lies the **Unwritten**, where nothing has been marked yet
and nothing can live. Ships that sail into the Margins come back if they come
back at all, with crews who remember the voyage as a blank page.

- **The Village Green** - the heart of common life: the well, the shrine, the
  smithy, the fire that is never allowed to die entirely. Villagers sleep
  better within sight of the green. They are right to.
- **Aldercastle** - raised in the Keeping to house and guard the Suncrest.
  Its keep's great hearth is the **Keep's Heart**, the stone's resting place.
  Its guards still swear to House Alder, a house with no living member, which
  they consider a technicality.
- **The Emberwood** - the eastern forest, grown thick where the ember fell.
  The ground there is thin: closer to the falling, closer to the burning.
  The oldest dead gather there, drawn to the warmth of the original site as
  moths to a window.
- **The First Camp** - the "old camp" of the eastern woods. Aldervale began
  here; when the castle was raised the people moved and left their first
  hearth to the trees. Every Keeper makes a pilgrimage to it once. It is
  where the Suncrest came from. It is where the Suncrest has been taken.
- **The Pass of Margins** - the only gap in the mountains, high in the north,
  where the fog thins enough to cross - some of the time, for some people.
- **The Shrine** - older than the castle, younger than the camp. Its crystal
  is the **First Mark**: the point the Chronicle was begun from. It holds a
  small bright copy - a mark - of every soul that has prayed at it. When one
  of the marked falls in the vale, the light carries what remains of them
  home to be re-inscribed. The villagers call this grace. Quill calls it
  editing.

## The History: Six Ages

**I. The Falling.** The First Fire breaks apart in the elder sky. Seven
embers land. The Emberwood is the scar of ours.

**II. The Founding.** Alder the First, a shepherd with an unexplained gift
for finding things, carries the ember out of the woods and refuses to let it
be sold, split, or worshipped. He lights a hearth at the First Camp. People
gather. The dark pulls back from the valley like a tide going out.

**III. The Keeping.** Alder dies in the fourth winter. The stone dims the
night he is buried, and the vale's first wolves come down out of the pass,
walking like men who have forgotten how. The surviving elders, in terror,
perform the first binding: Alder's spirit is sealed into the Suncrest at the
shrine. The stone warms. The wolves fall. The **Keepers' Watch** begins: the
Chronicle is opened, the castle is raised over the Keep's Heart, and the
rule is written - *the Keeper serves the vale in life and warms it in death.*
The office of **Champion of the Keep** is created: one knight, sworn to
protect the Keeper's living days, never to outlive their duty by much.

**IV. The Long Winter.** Three hundred years into the Keeping, the cold
collects. The **Grey** - the Winter Court, the patient intelligence of the
Unwritten - arrives to inquire after a debt it believes unpaid: fire, it
holds, is stolen property, and hearth-stones are theft from the dark. The
vale endures a season that lasts two years. The Watch holds because the
Keepers of that era had been generous with their living years - the stone was
fat with memory. The Grey withdraws, and leaves the vale a receipt: *it will
return each three hundred years, and each time it will count the fire and
count the debt.* The Quiet Ages are quiet because nobody does the arithmetic.

**V. The Quiet.** Generations of ordinary warmth. The Chronicle grows. The
price of the stone fades into liturgy, then into rumour, then into a figure
of speech. House Alder dwindles to a name on the gate. The Champion's oath
becomes ceremonial. Keepers still go into the stone, but the funerals are
called "retirements", and the stone's new warmth each time is called "a
blessing of the season."

**VI. The Fading (the present).** Maren the Kind, the eleventh Keeper, dies
in her sixty-first year and is bound according to the rite. Her husband -
Ser Corvin, Champion of the Keep, the man who carried her to the shrine
because he refused to let the priests touch her - stands witness. He later
testifies, to no one who wanted to hear it, that as the rite closed he felt
her **stop**, the way a voice stops: not ended - *filed*. He asks the
Chronicle's archivists to show him the pages that say where the kept souls
go. The pages say nothing. There are no pages. He asks what the Grey will
find when it next counts the fire. The answer is a polite silence, because
the solstice of the Winter Court's return is now within living memory.

Ser Corvin gives the vale one winter of warning. Then he takes the Suncrest
from the Keep's Heart, takes the Keeper's circlet from the shrine vault,
walks east into the Emberwood, and does not come back.

## The Present Crisis

The vale knows only this: the amulet is gone, the Captain wears a stolen
crown, and the dead walk at night. Here is what is actually true.

Corvin carried the Suncrest to the First Camp - where the ember began - to
perform the **Rite of Return**: an unbinding, worked at the place of
beginning, that would open the Suncrest and let every kept soul go out,
including Maren, and then give the ember back to the sky, closing the debt
and ending the Watch forever. The rite is slow. It requires the stone to sit
unkept while the binding is unpicked, strand by strand, in the order they
were laid - Alder first, Maren last.

While the Suncrest is unkept, its warmth gutters. The vale's dead - not evil,
only **unfinished** - feel the fire flicker and walk toward it, as they have
always walked toward warm things. The skeletons at the First Camp are not
Corvin's army. They are a siege he is enduring. He holds the rite-circle
with a greatsword and a dead man's patience, night after night, killing
things he believes he is trying to free.

The vale, reading darkness by firelight, sent for a hero.

Elder Quill - Keeper-presumpt, Maren's chosen successor, the next wood for
the fire - sent for **Ser Alaric**.

## Dramatis Personae

**Ser Alaric, the Margin Knight (the player).** Walked in through the Pass
of Margins one grey morning with a serviceable sword, a gentleman's manners,
and no past he can produce on request. The shrine marked him on arrival;
when he falls, the First Mark carries him home. What Quill has not told him:
the Pass opens for the vale's need, and the vale's need was specific. Quill's
predecessor, many years ago, copied a figure out of a drowned chronicle and
kept the copy in the margins of our own - a hero from a vale that no longer
exists, held in reserve against a winter. Alaric is a re-inscription. He does
not know which vale he was first written in. He may be somebody's second
draft. He fights as if he remembers being better at it once.

**Elder Quill, Keeper-presumpt.** Chronicler, widower of no one, husband of
the vale. Carries the staff whose crystal is a chip of the First Mark. Writes
the Chronicle nightly, and knows - has always known - what the next chapter
of the Keeping costs, because it is his name in it. Sent Alaric to retrieve
the Suncrest for the reasons he gave, and for one he did not: as long as the
stone sits unkept at the First Camp, the rite can be finished. As soon as it
is home above the Keep's Heart, every kept soul - Alder's, Maren's, all
eleven - is bound the deeper, and Quill's eventual binding becomes a matter
of correct paperwork. He is kind to Alaric. He is kind to everyone. It is
the only vice he permits himself. Each year his staff-crystal burns a shade
dimmer, and he writes a little smaller.

**Ser Corvin the Ashen, the Bone Captain.** Champion of the Keep, twice
over: first by oath, now by obstinacy. The circlet he wears is the Keepers',
not the lord's - the guards mistake it for the crown of dead House Alder,
and he has stopped correcting them. He is not raising the dead; he is
refusing to lower his sword until his wife is free. The Rite of Return has
already unpicked one strand of the binding: his own mortality. He no longer
bleeds, no longer sleeps, and no longer cares which of those came first. He
will ask Alaric, at the point of a greatsword, one question the game has not
yet let anyone answer: *"You have seen what the warmth is made of. Whose
name will you be wearing in it?"*

**Bram the Smith.** Forged the Keeper's circlet at the castle forge, forty
years ago, to the old specifications, and only later asked the archivists
what the specifications were *for*. He did not like the answer. He moved his
anvil to the village the following spring and has not entered the castle
since. He will not speak of the circlet. He pays generously for wolf pelts,
sells steel at a fair price, and hammers very late at night, because Bram
has begun - quietly, without telling anyone - to forge a second thing, of
which more in the open threads. His rule: metal remembers. He means this
technically.

**The Mourning Wyvern.** Not of this vale. It came over the pass some years
ago, circling, and has circled Aldercastle ever since. A wyvern bonds to a
hearth in its first year of life and outlives it by centuries; this one's
hearth was the **Mournstone** of **Hollowmere**, a vale across the mountains
whose Keeping failed - whose stone burned its last memory and went out. The
Mourning Wyvern is what is left guarding the ashes. It circles Aldercastle
because it can smell a hearth burning down to its memories, and it cannot
tell the difference between a stone that is dying and a stone that is being
opened. It is not hunting. It is **keeping vigil for the wrong grave.** It
has never once attacked the village. The chickens do not believe this.

**The villagers.** Mira of the Mill remembers when the Suncrest hung above
the Keep's Heart and the castle doors stood open - warmth makes a people
generous, and its absence makes them careful. Old Tam is older than he
admits and remembers the *last* time the east camp fires went strange; he
calls the Fading "the Winter counting again" and nobody listens, because
Tam also blames the miller's weathervane for things. Wren the Herder talks
to chickens and is right about the wyvern. Each of them holds one true page
of the story. The vale is a chronicle that has been read aloud so many
times it believes only the comfortable pages.

**The Grey (the Winter Court).** Not a monster. An **auditor**. The
Unwritten's patience given a schedule. It returns each three hundred years
to count the fire against the dark's ledger, and its coming solstice is the
clock under the whole tale. The Grey does not hate warmth. It objects to
warmth's accounting. Every soul bound into a hearth-stone is, in its ledger,
a debt recorded as an asset. The Rite of Return would close the account
honestly - which is why the Grey has not interfered with Corvin yet, and why
the honest ending frightens Quill more than the dark does.

## The Suncrest Itself

A disc of gold the size of a spread hand, warm as a held bird, always a
shade warmer than whoever holds it - which is the first clue, if anyone
thought about it, that it is holding *them*. Eleven Keepers are bound in it.
The warmth you feel through the metal is not radiance. It is **regard**.
Old Tam is right: the vale was warm when it hung above the hearth. The vale
was also, in the strictest sense, being worn.

## The Theme, Stated Once

Every fire in this world is a story someone is being spent to tell. The
Chronicle's answer - the saga's destination, held for the final act - is
that a hearth could be fed something else: that a **tale is a small sun**,
and a chronicle honestly kept, generously told, and often re-read might warm
a vale without costing anyone the way Alder was cost, and Maren, and Quill.
The last act of ALDERVALE, whenever it is written, is the vale learning to
burn its stories instead of its people - and the first proof that it works
is the game itself: a world made of nothing but marks, warm because someone
keeps writing it.

## Open Threads (for future acts)

1. **The Keeper's Price.** After the victory as currently played, the
   Suncrest burns brighter and Maren is bound the deeper. Quill's staff
   dims. Alaric begins finding pages in the Chronicle that mention him.
   The act ends with Alaric standing where Corvin stood, holding the same
   question.
2. **Bram's Second Forging.** The smith is working, nights, on a vessel
   meant to hold a hearth-stone *without* binding anyone - a lantern, a
   chronicle-box, a page of iron. It will work exactly once, at the worst
   possible moment.
3. **The Wyvern's Errand.** Win the Mourning Wyvern's trust (it judges by
   warmth of intent, not words). It carries a shard of the dead Mournstone.
   Follow it over the pass to Hollowmere, the vale that went dark, and learn
   what the Grey leaves behind when a Keeping fails - and what Alaric's
   first draft did there.
4. **The Winter Court Returns.** The solstice of the three-hundred-year
   audit. The Grey arrives counting. The vale must answer with a fire that
   owes nothing: the Chronicle itself, read aloud at the shrine, the First
   Mark as its hearth. The endgame of the theme.
5. **The Margins.** Beyond the fog, the Unwritten - where Alaric was first
   inscribed, where blankness is patient, and where the last page of the
   Chronicle has one unwritten line that Quill has been refusing to write
   for forty years.

## Rules for Future Story Writing

- Keep the fable cadence: short declarative sentences, one image per line,
  no explanations offered twice.
- Draw names from the lexicon of the page: mark, margin, quill, ember, ash,
  chronicle, strand, ledger, ink, vigil. Nothing is named accidentally.
- Never fully explain the glyphs. The world being made of tiny marks of
  light is canon, numinous, and not up for mechanistic revision.
- Every quest answers one question and opens two.
- The dead are not evil; they are unfinished. Every villain is someone's
  grief with a sword in it.
- Warmth, memory, and story are the same thing wearing three coats. When in
  doubt, the scene is about which one is being spent.
- The comfortable page is allowed to be true, and never allowed to be the
  whole truth.
